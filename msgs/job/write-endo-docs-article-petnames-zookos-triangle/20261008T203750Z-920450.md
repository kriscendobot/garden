# Findings brief: petnames and Zooko's triangle

## Corrections and additions the article most needs

- **Do not say Zooko proved an impossibility.** Verified against Zooko Wilcox-O'Hearn's version 0.9.1 essay (2001-10-12): he says he did not prove the result, frames it as a doubt about one namespace, and invites a design that specifies a universal ownership policy and justifies all three properties. Confirmed source: [Internet Archive original-bytes capture](http://web.archive.org/web/2001id_/http://zooko.com/distnames.html).
- **Use his original property meanings, not the wiki's loose paraphrase.** Verified against the same essay: distributed means no central authority controls the namespace, so it spans trust boundaries; secure means an attacker cannot force an incorrect lookup under a universal name-ownership policy; human-usable means people can read and remember the names. The original title is *Names: Distributed, Secure, Human-Readable: Choose Two*. The later “globally context-free / human meaningful / securely collision-free” labels come from Miller's PNML framing, not from Zooko's title. Verified against [Miller's mirrored PNML page](https://erights.github.io/erights-org-website/elib/capability/pnml.html).
- **Attribute petnames to Electric Communities, not to Stiegler or Close alone.** Verified against Jonathan S. Shapiro's [April 11, 2000 dcms-dev post](http://web.archive.org/web/20160507124033id_/http://www.eros-os.org/~majordomo/dcms-dev/0036.html), which credits “Mark Miller and others” at Electric Communities; Miller's PNML page, which says Communities.com developed the system for multi-way chat but had not documented it externally; and Marc Stiegler's [2005 HP report](http://web.archive.org/web/20220105063409id_/https://www.hpl.hp.com/techreports/2005/HPL-2005-148.pdf), which says petnames were invented at Electric Communities. Shapiro's message is the earliest public primary-source appearance located in this cycle, not necessarily the private invention date. Anything earlier is unverified.
- **Credit later roles separately.** Verified against Stiegler's report: Stiegler wrote the first broad external exposition located here, and Tyler Close was the first to recognize the phishing-defense use. Verified against Close's [2005 W3C workshop paper](https://www.w3.org/2005/Security/usability-ws/papers/02-hp-petname/): Close describes the theory as originating with Mark Miller and others at Electric Communities.
- **Present petnames as a layered workaround, not a counterexample to one global namespace.** Verified against Zooko's essay: his fourth alternative already proposes a loyal local agent translating self-authenticating global names into human-readable local names and links PNML. Verified against PNML and Stiegler: keys, nicknames or proposed names, and petnames cover different pairs of properties. A system can offer all three benefits collectively while no single global name has all three.

## Name kinds and Endo-facing vocabulary

- **Key or reference: what it is.** Verified against Stiegler: the machine-handled key is the globally unique, unforgeable designator and supplies forgery resistance. For Endo, keep this distinct from a human label. A locator or sturdy reference is a bearer designation/reference, not a petname. The last Endo-specific sentence is verified against the article job's stated code distinction and existing Endo source corpus, not against Stiegler.
- **Petname: what I call it.** Verified against Stiegler: it is private to one person and application, editable by that holder, and ideally a bidirectional one-to-one mapping to the key that the UI renders everywhere. Its security value depends on privacy and trusted UI, not on global uniqueness.
- **Nickname or self-proposed name: what it calls itself publicly.** Verified against Stiegler: an owner-selected nickname is a discovery hint, may map one-to-many, and has no secure collision guarantee. Verified against the 2022 Spritely paper at [the confirmed live source](https://files.spritely.institute/papers/petnames.html): a self-proposed name is the entity's preferred label.
- **Alleged or proposed name: what an introducer suggests.** Verified against Stiegler: an alleged name comes from a third party during introduction and can seed, but is not, the recipient's petname. Verified against the Spritely paper: a proposed name is introduced in local context.
- **Edge name: the label on a naming-graph edge.** Verified against the Spritely paper: each entity may act as a namespace, so `Uncle Bob -> Sarah Smith` uses `Sarah Smith` as Bob's edge name. An Endo sender's edge name is therefore a proposal carried on the delegation edge; it becomes the receiver's petname only if the receiver binds it locally. Do not collapse edge name, petname, and reference into synonyms.

## Critiques, counterexamples, and honest limits

- **The triangle is not a theorem.** Verified against Zooko's original explicit non-proof and invitation to counterexamples. Phrase it as a trilemma or design claim about one namespace.
- **DNS and HTTPS do not supply the decentralized corner.** Verified against Zooko's DNS pairing and the already-ingested 2011 cap-talk “Questions about Zooko's triangle” thread: memorable DNS names gain conditional secure uniqueness through DNS and CA authorities. CA compromise or jurisdictional compulsion can rebind a name. Library path: `library/sections/cap-talk-2009-2012--zookos-triangle-and-petname-mappings.md`.
- **PNML's “proves him wrong” line conflicts with its own construction.** Verified against PNML and Zooko: PNML agrees one name cannot carry all three, then combines several kinds of names exactly as Zooko's fourth alternative recommends. Treat this as conflicting rhetoric, not a demonstrated single-namespace counterexample.
- **The awesome-ocap wiki is too loose on “secure.”** Verified against [the wiki](https://github.com/dckc/awesome-ocap/wiki/PetNames) and Zooko: the wiki defines security as minimizing possible damage, while Zooko's claim is specifically about attacker-resistant lookup correctness under a universal ownership policy. Use the primary-source definition in the article.
- **Petname identity can hide reference provenance.** Verified against the already-ingested January 2009 cap-talk thread: E-order attaches ordering to the specific delegated reference, while an object-identifying petname can collapse a newly delegated reference and one already held. The thread does not resolve whether semantically different references should get different petnames. Library path: `library/sections/cap-talk-2009-2012--petnames-versus-e-order.md`.
- **Petnames do not solve discovery, shared vocabulary, global uniqueness, or trust transfer.** Verified against Stiegler. They localize the final human binding. Interfaces should introduce a reference plus a proposed label and let the receiver bind or rename it, rather than assume a shared lookup name.
- **The ideal one-petname/one-key model is simplified.** Verified against Stiegler: a human relationship may survive key rotation, one entity may have multiple keys, and one key may cover several organizational faces. The paper assumes one-to-one mapping for exposition.
