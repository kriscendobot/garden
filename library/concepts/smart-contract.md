# smart-contract

A **partially self-enforcing computational embodiment of a contract** — the term coined by Nick Szabo (1996) and adopted by the 2000 *Capability-Based Financial Instruments* paper as the natural use case for capability-based programming. In the capability framing, a smart contract is a composition of pre-existing primitives (purses, brands, timers, escrow purses, identity certificates) plus a small protocol-script that encodes the contract's specific terms. The §6.4 *CoveredCallOption* in the 2000 paper is the canonical worked example: composition of an `escrowedStock` purse + an `escrowedMoney` purse + a `timer` capability + brand discipline + state-machine logic, all built from the §3.4 mint-purse-money primitives. The deep architectural claim — central to the agoric vision — is that **every novel cooperative arrangement between mutually-suspicious parties does NOT require its own cryptographic protocol**. The cryptographic protocol design happens once (in Pluribus, the capability-transport substrate); each new contract is a *composition* of existing primitives, expressed in a simple security formalism *independent of cryptography*. The 2000 paper closes its abstract on this: *"we present cryptographic capabilities as an abstraction allowing a similar economy of engineering effort in creating smart contracts. We explain the E system, which embodies these principles, and show a covered-call-option as a smart contract written in a simple security formalism independent of cryptography, but automatically implemented as a cryptographic protocol coordinating five mutually suspicious parties."*

Smart contracts are *the* compositional payoff of capability discipline at the application layer. Agoric's *Zoe contract framework* is the production enactment: Zoe contracts are JavaScript modules running in Hardened JavaScript compartments, composing ERTP purses + amounts + brands + invitations + timers into self-enforcing arrangements. The 2000 paper's §6.4 CoveredCallOption + §6.5 TitleCompanyMaker (adding exclusivity by composition) are the structural blueprint Zoe operationalizes. The contemporary library's position: when a design names "smart contract" in an Endo / Agoric context, it means a Zoe-shaped composition of capability primitives, not a generic blockchain transaction.

## Common confusions

- **"Smart contract = blockchain."** Szabo's 1996 coinage *predates blockchains by 13 years*. A smart contract is any *partially self-enforcing computational embodiment of a contract*; blockchains are *one substrate* for hosting them. Capability-based smart contracts (the 2000 paper's framing) use *capability discipline* as the enforcement substrate, with Pluribus / OCapN as the wire protocol. Agoric Zoe is the production enactment that does *not* require a blockchain.
- **"A smart contract is just a script."** No — a smart contract is *a composition* of capability primitives (purses, brands, escrow purses, timers, invitations) plus a state-machine logic. The §6.4 CoveredCallOption code is ~30 lines, but it composes 4+ pre-existing capability primitives. The *script* is the contract-specific logic; the *primitives* are general.
- **"Smart contracts require AI / autonomous agents."** No — §6.4 CoveredCallOption is *deterministic*; it requires no AI. The smart-contract framing is about *automated enforcement* of contract terms (specifically, the property that the contract's success conditions can be checked algorithmically and the corresponding transfers happen atomically). AI-mediated contract drafting is a different (orthogonal) concern.
- **"Smart contracts require trusting the runtime."** Yes — but the trust is *bounded by visual-inspection-proof methodology*. The §3.4 mint-purse example's six security properties hold by code inspection; an analogous proof works for the §6.4 CoveredCallOption. The runtime trust is in the substrate (Hardened JavaScript / E / Pluribus); the contract logic itself is verifiable by visual inspection given that substrate.
- **"Smart contracts are slow because of cryptography."** §4.5 of the 2000 paper makes the opposite argument: *generic protocols, reusable security*. Each smart contract is *not* its own cryptographic protocol; instead a *single* cryptographic protocol (Pluribus) is implemented once, and smart contracts are composed in a higher-level security formalism. The cryptographic cost is paid once at the substrate level, not per-contract.
- **"Smart contracts must be public."** No — *capability-based smart contracts can be private*. A contract's existence is hidden by the structure of who holds references to it. Public-blockchain smart contracts are necessarily public (the blockchain is the substrate); capability-based smart contracts are private by default and public-only-when-references-are-public. This is one of the strongest properties capability-based smart contracts have that blockchain smart contracts do not.
