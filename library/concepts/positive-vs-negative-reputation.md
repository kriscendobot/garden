# positive-vs-negative-reputation

The **structural taxonomy** Miller-Drexler 1988 §5.3.3 establishes for reputation systems in agoric (or any distributed) settings:

- A **negative reputation system** identifies entities by their *bad* track record and avoids them. It is the conventional posture of credit-rating agencies, blocklist services, and informal "don't trust X" word-of-mouth. **Negative reputation systems fail if effective pseudonyms are cheaply available** — an entity with a bad reputation simply discards its identity and starts over under a new name.
- A **positive reputation system** identifies entities by their *good* track record and seeks them. It requires only that *one entity cannot claim the identity of another* — a condition met by *actors*, *public-key systems*, and (in the contemporary Endo / Agoric posture) per-agent keypairs.

The §5.3.3 architectural conclusion: **computational markets are expected to rely on *positive* reputation systems** because pseudonyms in distributed software are essentially free. A new participant with a fresh identity has *no* reputation — neither positive nor negative; only by accumulating positive evidence of good service can it acquire reputation that other agents will trust. The §5.3.3 paper also addresses the new-entrant cold-start problem: *new objects can give reason to expect good service — thereby establishing a positive reputation — by posting a cash bond guaranteeing good performance*. This requires both parties to the contract to trust some third party to hold the bond and judge performance. The contemporary cryptocurrency *staking* mechanism is a direct enactment of this 1988 cash-bond pattern.

The taxonomy compounds with the [[four-ways-to-acquire-references]] discipline: in object-capability systems, identity is established by the *holding of a reference to one's own keypair* (or equivalent unforgeable identifier), and reference-graph reachability is what makes positive reputation tracking tractable. ACL systems can in principle implement positive reputation, but the *cheap-pseudonym vulnerability* attaches to negative systems regardless of substrate; in object-capability systems, the negative-system vulnerability is largely moot because the substrate already supports unforgeable identity.

## Common confusions

- **"Negative reputation works if pseudonyms are expensive."** Yes — the §5.3.3 framing is *conditional*. Negative reputation systems work in domains where identity is costly to change (real-world commerce, credit ratings, professional licenses). The failure mode is specifically cheap-pseudonyms — a cost structure that *does* apply to most distributed software environments. The §5.3.3 conclusion that *computational* markets need positive reputation is specifically about the cost-of-pseudonyms feature of computational substrates.
- **"Positive reputation = whitelist."** Closer to *no, but related*. A whitelist is a *negative* reputation list (an explicit list of "those NOT-trusted"); a positive reputation system is a *judgement* about track records (a function from entity to score, not a binary). The architectural distinction matters because whitelists are also vulnerable to cheap-pseudonyms (a denied entity creates a new identity); positive scoring requires accumulating evidence over time.
- **"Cash bond is just crypto staking."** Crypto staking is *one* enactment of the §5.3.3 cash-bond pattern. The pattern is more general: any economic incentive that *survives* a bad-faith action and can be confiscated. Bail bonds, surety bonds, escrow deposits, performance guarantees — all instances of the same pattern. The 1988 paper anticipates the crypto-staking pattern by ~30 years.
- **"Reputation requires central coordination."** No — §5.3.3 explicitly addresses distributed reputation: *reputation systems within a community can extend [the iterated-relationship] principle and lower the overhead of using it. Some objects can gather and sell information on another object's past performance.* The Better Business Bureau / Underwriters Laboratories / Consumer Reports analogies apply in computational form. Each reputation service is itself an agent; the system as a whole composes without central coordination.
- **"Reputation = identity."** No — they are orthogonal. *Identity* is the substrate (unforgeable per-agent-keypair); *reputation* is the layer atop the substrate that tracks track records. The §5.3.3 framing requires identity to be unforgeable but does *not* require identity to be public or correlatable across services. A party can have a fresh identity in one context and a long-established identity in another; positive reputation accumulates per-identity.
- **"Cold-start is unsolvable."** §5.3.3 is explicit that the cash-bond pattern addresses cold-start. A new entrant *can* establish a positive reputation by demonstrating willingness to lose-the-bond if it acts in bad faith. The contemporary cryptocurrency staking pattern is the production form. The pattern requires a trusted bond-holder + a trusted judge; both can be filled by reputation services that themselves accumulated reputation via the same bootstrap.
