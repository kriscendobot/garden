# wanting-as-the-human-role

Chip Morningstar's thesis, from *Adventures In LLM Land* (Habitat Chronicles, 2026),
about **the irreducible human role in an AI-agent fleet**: the machine does the
mechanical heavy lifting, but the human must supply the **wanting** — deciding, and
articulating with completeness and precision, what is actually desired. The slogan
form is *"the AI can make things for you, but it can't want things for you"* and its
imperative close, *"Learn to be a good wanter."*

The claim has three load-bearing parts:

- **A division of labor.** The central design problem of building with AI agents is
  "maintaining the proper division of labor between you and the machine" — the machine
  "doing all the heavy lifting involved in making the mechanical parts," the human
  "doing the expressive parts." Morningstar's own [[habitat-unum]] essay uses the same
  phrase, *division of labor*, for how a distributed object's presences are factored
  (each authoritative about a different aspect); here the axis is human-versus-machine
  rather than presence-versus-presence, but the move is the same: partition authority
  by what each party is uniquely positioned to be authoritative about.

- **The AI wants nothing, so it can only guess your want.** Because the agent has no
  wants of its own, it can only guess at yours: a vague, mushy want yields slop, and a
  want articulated precisely enough to guarantee a good result is one you have
  effectively already built yourself. (You *can* outsource the wanting — but then "what
  you get is what *they* want"; the AI's apparent wants "largely reflect the wants of
  whoever set it up.")

- **Delegation passes down judgment and taste, not just tasks.** The seductive
  error — since a CEO already directs a flock of semi-autonomous actors, just "swap out
  humans for AIs in various boxes on your org chart" — breaks down because real
  delegation hands down *judgment and taste*: "knowing what you want at one level of
  abstraction does not imply that you know what you should want at another level." A
  complex organization runs on "many layers of recursively ramified desire" before
  anything concrete happens, and today's AI cannot hold the wanting at the intermediate
  layers (if it could, "they've probably become people themselves").

**Why it matters to the garden.** The garden is itself an agent fleet, and this concept
names the human-maintainer half of its contract. The gardeners supply the mechanical
labor; the maintainer (through the liaison) supplies the wanting — objectives, taste,
and precise-enough intent. The "recursively ramified desire" is not a hazard to avoid
but the literal shape of the fleet: top-level wanting enters through the maintainer,
the liaison and the role briefs refract it into progressively concrete objectives, and
only at the leaves does a gardener do something concrete. The warning is that the
*judgment at each layer* must be genuinely supplied — by a human where the taste is
human, or by a well-specified role brief where the objective can be made precise —
never hand-waved as "the AI will figure out what I want."

## Common confusions

- **"Wanting means just typing a prompt."** No — Morningstar's point is that a *vague*
  want yields slop; the wanting must be articulated "with completeness and precision,"
  which is itself hard human work (the "expressive part"), not a one-liner the AI
  fleshes out for you.
- **"An org chart of AIs is the same as a flock of AI agents."** No — this is the exact
  trap the essay names: a real hierarchy delegates *judgment and taste* through many
  intermediate layers, and today's AI cannot hold the wanting at those layers, so
  boxes-on-an-org-chart is not a valid substitution.
- **"This is anti-AI / AI-skeptic commentary."** No — Morningstar is "completely
  convinced this is just the way software development is going to be done henceforth";
  the concept is about *where the human labor moves to* (from writing code to wanting
  well), not about AI being useless.
