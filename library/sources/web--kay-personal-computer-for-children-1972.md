## Abstract

Alan Kay's 1972 DynaBook paper joins three arguments that later accounts often separate: children learn by constructing operational models in responsive environments; a personal computer should be an active, user-programmable medium rather than an automated instructor or remote terminal; and such a medium requires a uniform object-and-process language plus hardware cheap, portable, readable, and locally persistent enough to belong to its user. The paper moves from a worked scene of children extending a Spacewar simulation, through Piaget-, Moore-, and Papert-influenced educational principles, to a notebook-sized device, networked knowledge utilities, object/message semantics, component estimates, and an ownership-centered distribution model.
