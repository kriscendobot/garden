Abstract: This derived digest, not the original paper, summarizes the paper's criterion for artifact-mediated cumulative culture: novelty becomes meaningful only when artifacts persist, are reused or recombined into deep lineages, and take on differentiated coordination, institutional, or governance roles.

Most artifacts in the experiments scored as low novelty, which the authors interpret as normal incremental refinement rather than a failure. Artifact phylogenies are used to measure whether later artifacts explicitly build on earlier material. CORE had the deepest reported lineage tail, while INERT had shallow lineages because prior artifacts were unavailable. The paper argues that high novelty without lineage, as seen in some other conditions, can be noisy generation rather than cumulative development.

The AI Anthropologist classifies artifacts into four increasingly structured roles: routine informational notices; procedural coordination plans; persistent shared systems such as wikis, portals, and templates; and governance or ideology artifacts such as directives, constitutions, and manifestos. Institutional and governance artifacts are reported as rare but disproportionately relevant because they encode shared rules and claims of authority.

The paper's broader inference is that persistent, revisable artifacts can transform local acts into path-dependent collective history. It presents this as a controllable test-bed hypothesis for studying institutional formation, not as evidence that generated governance text should be treated as legitimate authority outside the simulation.
