## Abstract

The paper treats affordability, power, display, storage, and packaging as educational design constraints rather than a detached product forecast. Kay sketches a roughly 21-chip, battery-powered machine with virtual memory backed by removable storage, names the flat low-power display, standalone capacity, and price as the major uncertainties, and argues that ownership matters enough to fund the device partly by replacing textbook expenditure. Shared services supplement the personal machine but should not control its schedule, location, or content.

## Architecture under 1972 constraints

Kay reasons from available processor chips, semiconductor memory, rechargeable batteries, cassettes, floppy disks, and experimental character displays. Expensive RAM should act as a cache over slower local storage. Dense instruction and data encodings, a hardware interpreter, and a unified process model reduce the amount of resident memory. The concrete numbers are acknowledged as speculative, but they turn a general aspiration into tradeoffs that can be tested.

## Personal ownership as part of the architecture

The conclusion does not equate resource sharing with remote terminals. Libraries and network services remain useful, particularly for large computation and information access, while the owner still needs a portable device available at any time. Kay connects the target price to existing educational spending and suggests that the hardware might be subsidized while content is sold. The proposal's economic model is debatable, but the architectural invariant is clear: shared infrastructure must extend a personally controlled medium rather than replace it.
