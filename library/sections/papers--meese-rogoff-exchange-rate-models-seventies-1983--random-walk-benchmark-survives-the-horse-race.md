Abstract: Across currencies, forecast horizons, estimators, and error measures, no tested structural, univariate, multivariate, or forward-rate forecast reliably improves on using the current spot rate as the forecast. The paper's strongest implication is therefore methodological: a plausible structural explanation and acceptable in-sample fit do not establish an actionable directional forecast.

At the principal one- and twelve-month RMSE comparison, none of the candidates beats the random walk at any horizon. The structural models fail even though their forecast calculations use the realized future values of the explanatory variables, eliminating one common defense that the model is right but the inputs are unpredictable. Altering serial-correlation treatment, estimating in differences, changing money measures or expected-inflation proxies, adding separate domestic and foreign coefficients, or combining forecasts does not produce a stable winner. A few isolated improvements occur for particular currencies, horizons, or specifications, but they do not repeat across the evaluation grid.

MAE gives virtually the same ranking as RMSE, including for the estimator designed to minimize absolute deviations. The random-walk result also persists when the evaluation starts later or ends earlier. The forward rate performs no better overall, which the authors note may be consistent with a time-varying risk premium rather than a simple efficiency verdict. For a trading implementation, the right response is not to infer an eternal law from this historical sample. It is to require a pre-specified, cost-aware, live-like out-of-sample advantage over the random-walk or no-change baseline before converting a point forecast into directional exposure.
