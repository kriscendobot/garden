Abstract: Meese and Rogoff do not treat a failed horse race as proof that economics is irrelevant. They separate candidate explanations -- simultaneous-equation bias, sampling error, parameter instability, nonlinearities, risk premia, expectations proxies, purchasing-power-parity failures, and money-demand misspecification -- and conclude that no single repair is established. The durable result is narrower: these estimated seventies models did not provide an out-of-sample point-forecast improvement.

Instrumental variables, VAR estimation, and grids of theory-constrained coefficients fail to make sampling error or simultaneity a sufficient explanation, especially below twelve months. Parameter shifts around oil shocks, trade changes, and policy-regime changes may matter, and motivate methods that weight recent observations more heavily. The authors also test price-level substitutions for money variables and cross-rates that reduce dependence on unstable US money demand; those efforts do not overturn the broad ranking.

This is the boundary the financial-forecasting corpus needs. The paper supports [[efficient-market-hypothesis]] as an empirical caution against confidently trading exchange-rate direction from an in-sample structural narrative. It does not say every forecast target is equally unpredictable, and it does not refute conditional-volatility forecasting. A policy can therefore use validated volatility forecasts to scale risk while leaving expected directional return at a conservative baseline unless a separate, pre-specified evaluation supplies durable net-of-cost evidence.
