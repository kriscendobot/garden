## Abstract

Kay frames the personal computer as an active, portable medium for thought and creation, not as a teaching machine that substitutes automated drill for a skilled teacher. The DynaBook should let a child construct models, revise programs, retrieve knowledge, write, draw, and play, while remaining under the child's control. Technology is neither necessary nor sufficient for learning, but it can extend the book into a responsive medium whose user can also alter its tools.

## From teaching machine to active medium

The paper begins by rejecting technological cures that leave the sources of a social problem untouched. A conventional teaching machine can reproduce the behavior of a well-intentioned but untalented teacher. It cannot become an enlightened teacher merely by being cheaper or more automatic. The design goal must instead start from the learner: a child is an actor building practical models of the surrounding world, not a passive object to be programmed.

Kay's positive analogy is a piano. A personal computer can be a tool, toy, expressive medium, and source of pleasure, but its educational value depends on how people use it. Like the book, the new medium opens horizons without saving the world. Unlike a book, it can respond, simulate, and let its reader construct and transform the material.

## A worked learning scene

The Beth and Jimmy vignette shows two children modifying a Spacewar program to model gravity. Their game creates a reason to discover coordinates, vectors, and acceleration. The teacher diagnoses what they are ready to learn, offers a small hint, and points them toward a networked library. The children retrieve information, write a search filter, revise their simulation, test it, and then move between programming, play, and poetry on the same device.

The scene makes the division of labor concrete. The computer supplies a manipulable world and immediate feedback. The library supplies inherited knowledge. The teacher notices the learners' current model and gives a timely prompt. The children choose the goal and construct the next model themselves.
