## Abstract

Morningstar's warning about the seductive-but-wrong reading of "your job is to want
things": the belief that, because a CEO already directs a flock of semi-autonomous
actors, you can simply "swap out humans for AIs in various boxes on your org chart."
He grants the surface isomorphism — a CEO "gives general directions to a group of
semi-autonomous actors who then go and try to do whatever it is, under your guidance,"
which is structurally the same pattern as a human overseeing a flock of AI agents —
but argues the analogy **breaks down at delegation**: in a real hierarchy you delegate
not just the *performance of tasks* but *judgment and taste*, because "knowing what
you want at one level of abstraction does not imply that you know what you should want
at another level." A complex organization runs on "many layers of recursively
ramified desire" unfolding before anything concrete happens, and today's AI cannot
hold the *wanting* at the intermediate levels (if it ever could, "they've probably
become people themselves and then we're moving on to a whole new conversation"). The
essay closes on the corresponding human imperative — **"Learn to be a good wanter"** —
and the caution that the qualities that lead to success shift with every paradigm
change. Directly germane to how the garden layers delegation: the maintainer supplies
top-level wanting, the liaison and roles refract it into progressively concrete
objectives, and the gardeners execute — the recursive-ramified-desire structure made
into a fleet. See [[wanting-as-the-human-role]].

## Content

Morningstar first establishes the surface match between running a company and
running an agent flock. A CEO's control "is largely indirect" — "micromanagement
doesn't scale" — so the job "consists of establishing a vision and a purpose, and a
strategy to achieve these, and then trying to communicate all this to the rest of the
company, so that people in different parts of the organization can align what they do
without constantly having to explicitly coordinate." He then poses it as a
recognition test:

> So, to summarize: you're a person who (hopefully) has an idea in their head of what
> they want, which might be something big and complicated. You give general
> directions to a group of semi-autonomous actors who then go and try to do whatever
> it is, under your guidance. Does this pattern sound familiar?

And immediately names the trap:

> From there it's a pretty small step to thinking you can swap out humans for AIs in
> various boxes on your org chart. They sound like the same thing, right? I can see
> how this might be seductive, but it's also likely to be a terrible mistake.

The reason it is a mistake is the nature of delegation itself:

> The analogy breaks down because a CEO has to delegate the wanting of things to
> lower levels in the organizational hierarchy — one of the things you are delegating
> is not just the performance of tasks but judgement and taste. Even if you are
> brilliant as a CEO, you might well lack a deep sense of what's good or bad in the
> realm of, say, graphic design or advertising strategy. Knowing what you want at one
> level of abstraction does not imply that you know what you should want at another
> level to get to your desired end state. In a complex organization, there may be
> many layers of recursively ramified desire unfolding before reaching the point of
> doing something concrete, like writing a piece of code or purchasing a building or
> moving things around in a warehouse or ordering parts to build some widget.

He reinforces it with the "most of us aren't Steve Jobs" point: a weak-product-vision
executive can't hand the AI "conceive of a product ... that lots of people will want"
("too vague, and yet at the same time too anchored in the particulars of what your
company is already doing" — "instead, you have people for that"), and even a genuine
Steve Jobs "does not have the breadth of talent to do all the things" nor "the
capacity for the sheer quantity of creative work." The delegated wanting at the
intermediate layers is irreducibly human — at least until AIs "*can* handle a
directive of this sort, but at that point I think they've probably become people
themselves and then we're moving on to a whole new conversation."

The essay's closing turns this into an imperative for the individual practitioner.
Having been "habituated to a world in which the major productivity bottleneck was our
own capacity to write and debug code," developers now find that bottleneck suddenly
widened — "like stepping on the clutch and the gas pedal at the same time." The new
role is wanting:

> We need to learn how to accommodate ourselves to our new role, which is wanting
> things. It will help to have good taste and good judgement ... As with every
> paradigm shift, the qualities that lead to success are going to change from what
> they historically were.

He warns against fatalism ("don't fall into the trap that thinking these qualities
are fixed and immutable, that you are either one of the elect or you are not") — much
of what one is good at "follows from the habits of thought that you have learned and
practiced," which can be relearned. The final line:

> Learn to be a good wanter.

For the garden, the "recursively ramified desire" is not a hazard to avoid but the
literal shape of the fleet: top-level wanting enters through the maintainer, the
liaison and the role briefs refract it into progressively more concrete objectives,
and only at the leaves does a gardener do something concrete. Morningstar's warning is
the reminder that the *judgment at each layer* is what must be genuinely supplied —
by a human where the taste is human, and by a well-specified role brief where the
objective can be made precise — not hand-waved as "the AI will figure out what I
want."
