Abstract: The paper that introduced the **HAR-RV model** (Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of Realized Volatility), now the workhorse forecaster for realized volatility. It takes the realized-volatility measurement idea of [[garch-volatility-models]] (Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold & Labys 2003) and asks: once volatility is an observable series, what model forecasts it well without the fragility of true long-memory (ARFIMA/FIGARCH) machinery? Corsi's answer is an additive **volatility cascade** motivated by the Heterogeneous Market Hypothesis (traders act on daily, weekly, and monthly horizons), which reduces after recursive substitution to a single linear regression of tomorrow's daily realized volatility on the past daily, weekly, and monthly averages of realized volatility. That is HAR(3)-RV: three regressors, ordinary least squares, no fractional-difference operator. Despite being formally a short-memory model, it reproduces the long-memory autocorrelation decay, fat tails, and self-similarity of financial data, and out of sample it steadily beats short-memory AR models and matches the far more cumbersome ARFIMA on USD/CHF, S&P500, and T-Bond futures. Its parsimony and one-line OLS estimation are why HAR is the standard realized-volatility baseline any newer model must beat (the natural QLIKE / [[walk-forward-validation]] comparison).

## What the paper contributes

By 2009 the realized-volatility program (see [[garch-volatility-models]], Andersen et al. 2003) had established that summing squared high-frequency intraday returns yields a nearly error-free ex-post estimate of a day's volatility, and that modeling this observed series directly beats GARCH and stochastic-volatility models out of sample. The open question was *which* time-series model to fit. The empirical volatility series shows **long memory**: its autocorrelations decay slowly (hyperbolically), persisting for months. The standard tool for long memory is fractional integration (ARFIMA models of realized volatility, FIGARCH of returns), but Corsi argues fractional integration is a "convenient mathematical trick" that lacks a clear economic interpretation, is hard to estimate (biased GPH estimates of the fractional coefficient d, sensitivity to Taylor-expansion cutoffs), loses many observations to its build-up period, and does not extend cleanly to the multivariate case.

Corsi's move follows an alternative view (LeBaron 2001; Granger 1980): apparent long memory can be an artifact generated by a *short*-memory process built from a few components at different time scales. If the sampled aggregation range is not large relative to the slowest component's half-life, a superposition of a few AR(1) processes is statistically indistinguishable from genuine hyperbolic decay. LeBaron showed three AR(1) components suffice. So instead of a long-memory model, Corsi proposes a parsimonious three-component model that only *looks* long-memory over the horizons that matter, and is trivial to estimate.

## The model in one line

HAR(3)-RV regresses next-day realized volatility on three lagged realized-volatility averages -- daily, weekly (5-day), and monthly (22-day):

```
RV(d)_{t+1d} = c + beta_d * RV(d)_t + beta_w * RV(w)_t + beta_m * RV(m)_t + omega_{t+1d}
```

where `RV(w)_t` and `RV(m)_t` are simple averages of the daily realized volatilities over the past week and month. Because the monthly term spans 22 trading days, HAR(3) is exactly a *restricted* AR(22): 22 lags collapsed onto a three-step-function weighting scheme with only three free coefficients. Estimation is ordinary least squares with a Newey-West serial-correlation correction. Full derivation in [the-har-rv-model-as-restricted-ar](papers--corsi-simple-long-memory-model-realized-volatility-2009--har-rv-model-as-restricted-ar.md); the economic story in [heterogeneous-market-hypothesis-and-volatility-cascade](papers--corsi-simple-long-memory-model-realized-volatility-2009--heterogeneous-market-hypothesis-and-volatility-cascade.md).

## Why it matters for a forecasting system

The headline empirical result (see [out-of-sample-forecast-performance](papers--corsi-simple-long-memory-model-realized-volatility-2009--out-of-sample-forecast-performance.md)): out of sample, HAR(3) steadily outperforms AR(1)/AR(3) at one-day, one-week, and two-week horizons and is comparable to ARFIMA, while being "extremely simple and straightforward to implement even on a daily moving window" where ARFIMA is "cumbersome and complicated." The gap over short-memory AR models grows at longer horizons, because AR(1)/AR(3) revert to the unconditional mean too quickly. This combination -- near-long-memory forecasting accuracy from three OLS regressors -- is why HAR is the default realized-volatility baseline, and why the many HAR extensions (jump-robust HAR-CJ, leverage HAR, HARQ, and multivariate Vector-HAR) all keep the same additive-cascade skeleton.
