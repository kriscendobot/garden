Abstract: Close proposes the Granovetter introduction as the atom from which trust is built: every reputation is an aggregation of communications that connected a recipient to an entity and attached some claim. Finney and Wagner test the model against gradual human familiarity, guessed domain names, and chance encounters. The resulting refinement is that introductions need not be deliberate endorsements. Physical proximity, a shared world, and infrastructure can create connectivity without a trusted third party or a value judgment. Sparse cryptographic spaces make the rule "only connectivity begets connectivity" much stronger than it is in densely populated human namespaces.

## Trust aggregates introductions

A recommendation contributes only the confidence assigned to that communication. Repeated advertisements, purchases, and reports can collectively create a strong reputation even when each introduction is weak. Object identity matters to the aggregation: if two references cannot be tested as the same entity, they accumulate as two weaker relationships rather than one stronger relationship.

## Encounter is not endorsement

The thread resists treating every meeting as a conventional Alice-introduces-Bob-to-Carol event. Two strangers can meet on a bus without a person choosing to introduce them. The proposed reconciliation treats the bus, physical senses, or shared world as the connectivity mechanism. That mechanism supplies reachability, not a claim that either stranger is trustworthy.

## Sparseness is the security boundary

Random human encounters and guessed telephone numbers are plausible because their spaces are dense. E swiss numbers and YURL fingerprints inhabit sparse spaces where random discovery is negligible. The object-capability model gains confinement by restricting the shared world and controlling introductions; it should not import stronger conclusions about sparse references into ordinary human naming without stating that condition.
