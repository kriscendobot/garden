## Abstract

Kay derives the DynaBook's educational requirements from Moore, Papert, Piaget, Montessori, and related work: treat the child as a capable creator and explorer, permit frequent changes of role, make experimentation socially and physically safe, respond immediately, and ensure that each learned operation becomes material for further work. Programming serves "Computer Aided Intuition" because learners use ideas for self-chosen ends rather than rehearsing answers for later tests.

## The child as an active agent

The paper attributes short attention less to an inability to concentrate than to the strain of remaining in one relation to an idea. A learner may need to alternate among listener, active agent, judge, and game player. A clarifying environment therefore offers many perspectives and lets the learner form a model both of the subject and of their own activity.

Such an environment is safe enough for ungraded experiments, productive enough that old ideas become components of new ones, and responsive enough that action quickly reveals consequences. Serious public tests of skill still have a place, but they cannot replace private trial, error, and revision.

## Learning by doing something worth doing

Kay contrasts programmed instruction with environments in which children program animations, games, graphics, and music for purposes they recognize. Arithmetic becomes useful when multiplication changes the size of a drawing. Geometry becomes useful when a simulation needs direction and motion. Each artifact is a satisfying subgoal that rewards partial mastery while creating a reason to acquire more skill.

The resulting measure of progress is not answers per test. It is the learner's growing ability to imagine, construct, debug, and refine meaningful work. The computer belongs in this argument because it can host many media and because the child can program it, not because automation itself teaches.
