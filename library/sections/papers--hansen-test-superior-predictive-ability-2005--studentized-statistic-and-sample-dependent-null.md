Abstract: The two theoretical modifications that make SPA more powerful than the Reality Check and robust to padding the model set with junk. **Modification 1, studentization:** the Reality Check statistic `T^{RC}_n = max_k n^{1/2} d̄_k` maximizes over *un-scaled* average relative performances, so a single erratic (high-variance) alternative can dominate the maximum and conceal a genuine but lower-variance winner. Hansen studentizes: `T^{SPA}_n = max[max_k (n^{1/2} d̄_k / ω̂_k), 0]`, dividing each by its own standard error `ω̂_k`. His worked local-alternative example (`m = 2`, one informative alternative centered at a `t`-statistic of 2, one pure-noise alternative with four times the variance) shows studentization raising 5%-level power from about 15% to about 53% — "the noisy `d̄_1` conceals the evidence against `H0` that `d̄_2` provides." Studentization is the conventional multiple-comparison approach (Miller 1981; Savin 1984) and the same one Romano & Wolf (2005) adopt. The only case where it can *lose* power is when the best model also has the largest variance, which Hansen argues is empirically rare because poor models tend to be the erratic ones. **Modification 2, the sample-dependent null distribution:** instead of the LFC's blanket `µ = 0`, use a data-dependent estimator `µ̂^c` that identifies and down-weights the poor/irrelevant alternatives while keeping *all* of them in the analysis.

**Why the LFC is manipulable (Theorem 1 and Corollary 1).** Hansen derives the asymptotic null distribution for a whole *class* of test statistics `T_n = φ(U_n, V_n)` satisfying regularity conditions (Assumption 2: `φ ≥ 0`, `φ(0, v) = 0`; only positive elements of `u` matter; `φ → ∞` as evidence grows). The key object is `Ω^0`, a version of the asymptotic covariance `Ω` in which the rows/columns for alternatives with `µ_k ≠ 0` are zeroed out — so **only the binding constraints (`µ_k = 0`) matter for the asymptotic null distribution.** Corollary 1 makes this concrete for the Reality Check: `T^{RC}_n` converges to the maximum of `m_0` correlated normals, where `m_0` is the number of alternatives that exactly tie the benchmark. An immediate consequence: the RC's p-value, based on `max(Z_1, …, Z_m)`, "can be increased in an artificial way by adding poor forecasts" — raising `m` while `m_0` stays fixed inflates the critical value and "it is possible to erode the power of the RC to 0 by including poor alternatives." `T^{SPA}_n` satisfies Assumption 2 (the RC statistic itself does not, but `max(T^{RC}_n, 0)` does, and coincides with `T^{RC}_n` on the positive axis that matters).

**The `µ̂^c` estimator and the law-of-iterated-logarithm threshold.** The sample-dependent estimator is
`µ̂^c_k = d̄_k · 1{ n^{1/2} d̄_k / ω̂_k ≤ −√(2 log log n) }, k = 1, …, m`.
An alternative's estimated mean is set to its sample value only when its `t`-statistic falls *below* the threshold `−√(2 log log n)` (i.e. it looks clearly inferior); otherwise it is treated as binding (`µ̂^c_k = 0`). The threshold is motivated by the **law of the iterated logarithm**: it is the *slowest* rate that still captures every truly-binding (`µ_k = 0`) alternative asymptotically (`µ_k = 0 ⇒ µ̂^c_k = 0` a.s.), while any genuinely poor alternative (`µ_k < 0`) eventually falls below it (`µ_k < 0 ⇒ µ̂^c_k ≪ 0` a.s.). Crucially, the poor alternatives are **not simply discarded** — a model only moderately worse than the benchmark (`t`-statistic between about −1 and 0) still influences the critical value in finite samples, so omitting the `d̄_k < 0` models entirely would give invalid inference. Hansen explicitly rebuts an earlier misquotation: the method does *not* "discard the poor models," it keeps all of them while reducing the influence of those the data flag as poor. (Faster threshold rates such as `¼ n^{1/4}` also give valid tests; the range of valid rates is what produces the p-value bounds below.)

**Consistent p-value and the l/c/u bounds (Theorem 2, Corollary 2).** Three estimators bracket the choice: `µ̂^l_k = min(d̄_k, 0)` (lower), the data-dependent `µ̂^c`, and `µ̂^u_k = 0` (upper — the LFC), with `µ̂^l ≤ µ̂^c ≤ µ̂^u`. They yield null distributions `F^l_n ≤ F^c_n ≤ F^u_n` and hence a **lower, consistent, and upper p-value**. Theorem 2 shows `µ̂^c` gives a *consistent* estimate of the asymptotic null distribution (`F^c_n → F_0`); Corollary 2 shows the studentized statistic with `µ̂^c` yields a **consistent p-value** `p̂^c_n(t) → p_0(t)`. The other two do not: `µ̂^u` (the LFC / Reality Check) is inconsistent unless `µ = 0`, and `µ̂^l` gives an *anti-conservative* (liberal) p-value — smaller than the truth — a boundary-of-the-parameter-space inconsistency related to Andrews (2000). The practical payoff of reporting `p̂^l` and `p̂^u` alongside `p̂^c`: a **substantial gap between the lower and upper p-values is itself a diagnostic that poor alternatives are present**, which is exactly when the sample-dependent null distribution earns its keep.
