Abstract: Changing the horizon, constraining forecasts, adding longer-memory ratios, or correcting persistent regressors can produce isolated better results, but it also enlarges the search space. Welch and Goyal show why a researcher cannot promote the best-looking variant after the fact as a durable signal: most monthly and alternative specifications still fail in sample or out of sample, and choosing the few exceptions requires hindsight.

For monthly forecasts, Campbell and Thompson's safeguards truncate a negative predicted equity premium at zero and can substitute the unconditional forecast when an estimated coefficient has the wrong theoretical sign. Those choices improve some rows, but they also turn a linear forecast into a constrained nonlinear strategy. Without further manipulation, equity issuance is the only variable with both superior in-sample and OOS performance; several others improve after the constraints, often with nontrivial turnover or still-negative certainty-equivalent gains. The paper stresses that a practical investor would have needed to know beforehand which models survived, while none maintained superior performance over the last three decades.

The alternative-specification exercise makes the multiple-testing problem concrete. The authors try 90 dividend and earnings ratio combinations with different memories, then focus a table on the 27 significant in-sample cases. Only five have positive and significant OOS performance, none works over the recent three decades, and the investor-selection question remains. More powerful corrections for persistent predictors, learned shrinkage, model selection, and encompassing tests do not produce a general improvement. New transformations or model classes are legitimate hypotheses, but their apparent success must await fresh data rather than being certified on the searched history.
