Abstract: The paper's portable conclusion is a burden of proof for return-prediction claims: a forecast should show stable, genuinely superior in-sample and out-of-sample performance on data after the model was identified. In the authors' 2005 endpoint, most prominent annual-or-shorter-horizon equity-premium models do not clear that burden and generally underperform the prevailing mean.

Welch and Goyal call their test conservative, not permissive: they select predictors already published and thus let the data used to establish them remain in the evaluation history. Even under that advantage, most models lose statistical significance and fail to beat the unconditional benchmark. Removing the Oil Shock generally makes results weaker. More elaborate searches can find a favorable configuration by chance, so sophistication itself increases the need for a held-out future period.

For a forecasting system this is a scope boundary. Do not infer that a predictor with historical regression significance supplies tradeable expected-return timing. Log the benchmark, forecast origin, estimation window, all candidate configurations, costs and turnover, and cumulative OOS performance by subperiod. If the conditional forecast cannot robustly beat the historical mean after selection, retain the mean forecast or shift effort to the more empirically tractable task of volatility forecasting and exposure sizing.
