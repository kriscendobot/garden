Abstract: The talk reports interview-based believability comparisons and three social outcomes—information diffusion, relationship formation, and event coordination—while also naming location errors, failures of implicit norms, overly formal/cooperative language, and rapidly growing inference cost. The evidence supports architecture-sensitive coherence in this bounded simulation, not a general claim of human-equivalent social understanding.

Participants ranked agent answers to questions about self-knowledge, memory, plans, hypothetical reactions, and reflection. The comparison included the full observation/reflection/planning architecture, progressively ablated variants, and human-authored answers. In the simulation, news of a candidacy reached 32% of agents, a party invitation reached 52%, reported relationship-network density rose from 0.167 to 0.74, and 5 of 12 invitees attended the party.

The deck's limitations qualify those results. As memory accumulated, agents sometimes selected atypical locations; they misread implicit physical and social norms; instruction tuning encouraged excessive formality and cooperativeness; and inference cost grew sharply with population size. Proposed questions include whether fine-tuning helps, whether emergence replicates across environments and population sizes, and whether longer simulations preserve believable behavior.
