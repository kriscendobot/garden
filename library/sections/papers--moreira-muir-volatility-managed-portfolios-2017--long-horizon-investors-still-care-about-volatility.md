Abstract: A long investment horizon can attenuate the response to volatility caused by temporary discount-rate shocks, but it does not generally justify ignoring volatility. Under the paper's calibrations, even long-horizon investors retain a substantial volatility-managed component because price mean reversion is slow and some volatility reflects permanent cash-flow risk.

The dynamic model separates expected-return and volatility states and distinguishes cash-flow shocks, which permanently affect wealth, from discount-rate shocks, which can mean-revert. A short-horizon mean-variance investor uses the inverse-variance policy directly. A long-horizon investor has hedging demands, but the paper finds that the optimal policy is closely approximated by a constant-weight combination of passive buy-and-hold and the volatility-managed portfolio.

The allocation to the managed component depends on what drives volatility. If volatility rises through permanent cash-flow shocks, a long-horizon investor can respond more aggressively than a short-horizon investor. If it rises through transitory discount-rate shocks, the response is weaker, yet the authors' 30-year calibrations still retain meaningful volatility timing because expected-return mean reversion is persistent. The operational lesson is to state horizon and the shock model explicitly, rather than use "long term" as an exemption from risk control.
