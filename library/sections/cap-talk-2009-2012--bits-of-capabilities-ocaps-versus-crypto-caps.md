Abstract: A crisp statement of one of object-capability theory's load-bearing distinctions. David Nicol described his web system's caps as unguessable Crockford-encoded 80-bit numbers; Norman Hardy resisted "the drift towards forgetting past capability systems," pointing out that classic capability systems do *not* rely on hiding the bits of a capability — they hide bits only for other reasons (virtualization) — and that to know a cap's bits is "neither necessary nor sufficient to hold the cap." In KeyKOS the caps you hold live in storage that belongs to you but from which you cannot fetch or store bits, much as a Unix file descriptor gives no read access to the kernel bits defining it; KeyKOS provides a closely-held capability to *see* a cap's bits only so that cap-indexed maps can be built. Mark Miller named the two things being conflated: **object-capabilities (ocaps)** are *unforgeable*, whereas **cryptographic capabilities (crypto-caps)** are merely *unguessable*. On an open network between mutually suspicious machines, the best you can do is crypto, so the best cap system is crypto-caps — genuinely weaker, because their access is only knowledge-limited: a crypto-cap can be transmitted over a bit-only channel (you can copy the number), whereas an ocap cannot (there is nothing to copy). Miller's sharpest consequence: **Boebert's "impossibility" is actually impossible for crypto-caps** — the confinement/*-property (permitting low-to-high communication while preventing high-to-low) that an ocap platform can enforce cannot be enforced when caps are just transmissible numbers. He cited section 11.5 of his thesis, "The Limits of Decentralized Access Control," for the full list of what a shared platform can do that an open network of mutually suspicious machines cannot. Terminology remained slippery: Miller favored "sparse capability" (Amoeba's term) for the choose-a-large-unguessable-number technique, and flagged the Monash "password capabilities" as a taxonomy-breaking half-way point (only unguessable, yet somehow resisting Boebert's attack — but only on a *shared* platform, so the open-network limit still holds).

## Unforgeable is stronger than unguessable

The distinction is not pedantry; it fixes what each kind of capability can and cannot guarantee. An ocap is a reference the runtime hands you and that you cannot manufacture, copy as bits, or transmit as data — its unforgeability is a property of the platform's reference machinery, not of any secret. A crypto-cap is a bearer secret: whoever learns the number holds the authority, so it *is* copyable and transmissible, and its security is only that the number is hard to guess. This is why Hardy insists the bits are irrelevant to holding an ocap (holding is a platform fact, not a knowledge fact) while for a crypto-cap the bits are the *whole* thing. The practical upshot is Miller's: on a single trusted platform you can build ocaps and get the strong confinement properties (the *-property, controlled information flow); the moment you cross an open network between mutually suspicious machines you fall back to crypto-caps and *lose* those properties, because a transmissible secret cannot be confined the way an unforgeable reference can. The whole design space of distributed capability systems is a negotiation of this boundary — ocaps within a vat, crypto-caps (swissnums, sparse caps) across the wire.

## Bearing on Endo

This is the exact seam Endo is built on. *Inside* a vat, Endo capabilities are ocaps: unforgeable object references the confined evaluator hands out, holding nothing you could serialize and resend, so Endo gets the strong in-vat confinement Miller describes. *Across* a `captp` connection between mutually suspicious peers, a reference is represented by a swissnum-style unguessable identifier — a crypto-cap — because, as Miller says, crypto is the best you can do on an open network. Endo's marshal layer is precisely the translation between the two regimes: an ocap on one side becomes a crypto-cap on the wire and is re-presented as an ocap on the far side, and the security downgrade Miller names (knowledge-limited, not permission-limited; no *-property across the wire) is the reason Endo cannot promise open-network confinement and does not pretend to. Hardy's "the bits are neither necessary nor sufficient to hold an ocap" is why Endo never treats a serialized reference as itself the authority in-vat, and why leaking a wire identifier is a crypto-cap disclosure with exactly the transmissibility Miller warns of.
