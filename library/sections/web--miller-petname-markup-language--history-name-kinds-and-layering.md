Abstract: Mark S. Miller's *PetName Markup Language* page is a derived source, not the original, and provides the most explicit origin narrative: Communities.com developed the PetName system for multi-way chat; the system itself was not externally documented; and the April 2000 dcms-dev messages were the brief public antecedents. It also sharpens Zooko's three properties to globally context-free, human-meaningful, and securely collision-free, then combines keys, nicknames, and private petnames rather than claiming one name has every property.

The page defines a cryptographic key as a global, secure, self-authenticating designator. A nickname is a public label presented as a property of the named entity. A petname is private to the relationship between a namer and the named entity. Petnames therefore belong to a local namespace and translate to a secure designator; an introduction may carry a suggested human label, but the recipient's local binding determines the petname.

The page says Zooko's essay brought these ideas into the framework later called Zooko's Triangle. Its wording that PNML “proves him wrong” conflicts with the actual construction: the page immediately agrees that no single name has all three properties and instead uses three kinds of names covering the three pairs. Zooko's essay already lists a local translating agent, linked to PNML, as its fourth alternative. PNML is therefore an implementation of the workaround, not a counterexample to the single-namespace claim.

The historical attribution is collective. Miller names Eric Messick, John Sullivan, Trevor Morris, Bill Frantz, Ellen Isaacs, and perhaps Randy Farmer as contributors or influences, while especially crediting Zooko Wilcox-O'Hearn, Tyler Close, Marc Stiegler, and Alan Karp for later development. Stiegler's paper gives the concise attribution: invented at Electric Communities, with Tyler Close first recognizing the anti-phishing use.
