Abstract: The paper's mechanism is not that volatility forecasts reveal high future returns. It is that a predictable variance denominator moves substantially while the conditional-mean numerator moves only weakly, so inverse-variance sizing improves a mean-variance investor's trade-off more reliably than expected-return timing in the studied data.

The authors contrast a conditional-mean signal with a variance signal. For the market, a simple lagged-variance forecast has substantial reported explanatory power for log variance, whereas expected-return timing has much weaker evidence and is vulnerable to parameter instability. In their calculations, an AR(1)-style variance forecast produces an economically large gain relative to an unconditional allocation even after allowing that expected return and variance can co-move. The portfolio conclusion follows only when higher expected returns fail to compensate proportionally for higher variance.

This is a disciplined interpretation of the efficient-market evidence. It does not claim that expected returns never vary or that every inverse-volatility rule is superior. It says a system should treat volatility forecasting as the primary dependable input, estimate mean timing skeptically on a fresh holdout, and evaluate the complete sizing policy with the same information timing and cost assumptions it would have live.
