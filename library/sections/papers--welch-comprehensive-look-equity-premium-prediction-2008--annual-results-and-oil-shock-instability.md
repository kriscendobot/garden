Abstract: On annual horizons, no single predictor satisfies the paper's four investor-facing requirements together: in-sample significance, positive out-of-sample performance, no reliance on a small unusual episode, and good performance in the most recent three decades. The apparent success of several predictors is concentrated in the 1973-1975 Oil Shock, with no model performing well thereafter in the authors' sample.

The annual tables distinguish variables that are not significant even in sample from variables that are. High in-sample R2 does not rescue a forecast: the kitchen-sink regression has strong in-sample significance but exceptionally poor out-of-sample performance. Equity issuance is the one annual variable with statistically significant full-period OOS performance, but its advantage is concentrated around the Oil Shock and it underperforms over the recent 30 years. Variables such as book-to-market, investment-to-capital, dividend yield, and earnings-price may have an attractive historical interval, yet do not clear the complete test.

The cumulative-error plots make the time dependence visible. A rising line means the predictor beat the mean; a falling line means the mean won. The 1973-1975 recession supplies many models' favorable observations. The authors do not declare that episode irrelevant, because an investor cannot know beforehand which shock should be ignored. Their narrower conclusion is that the long post-1975 failure is the decisive warning: a result whose useful period cannot be identified ex ante is not a robust market-timing rule.
