Abstract: A December 2010 reading of Van Jacobson et al.'s *Networking Named Content* (CoNEXT 2009), the founding Content-Centric Networking (CCN) paper, through the capability/self-authenticating-names lens. Sandro Magi flagged two things: the design "pushes some computation into the network itself, as clients can send along small query programs to precisely specify the exact data they want" (which he read as "shades of promise pipelining"), and its Section 5.2.2 "Evidence-Based Security" — "web-keys and Tyler's httpsy scheme implement exactly these semantics," prior work CCN does not cite. **Zooko O'Whielacronx** gave the decisive critique. On CCN's Section 5.1 "Content Validation": CCN argues self-authenticating names are insufficient for usability (not user- or application-meaningful) and roots user-chosen names *within the scope of a public key* — but that "means their approach **is** a self-authenticating name system, the same as Self-certifying Filesystem and as Tahoe-LAFS," so CCN reinvents the very category it claims to improve on. On the "Evidence-Based Security" section: Zooko agreed CCN's statement of *delegation* ("publisher P of a link named N with target (N?,P?) intends N to refer to whatever P? refers to by N?") is "exactly right, and deeply important," but faulted the paper's slide into a *transitive-trust* frame — that having decided to trust page A "users may automatically trust the content A securely links to" — as adding no meaning beyond the **y-property**: "if you are viewing A, a web page, and it refers to an image, then the image you get is one that was acceptable to the web page." He called the paper's further idea — comparing two names rooted in *different* public keys but sharing a relative path (Alice's `web_pages/front_page.html` vs Bob's) to derive information — "senseless," because sharing a relative path tells you nothing beyond what each namer meant by their own name.

## Zooko's two corrections

**1. CCN's content validation *is* self-authenticating naming.** CCN's Section 5.1 opens by arguing against self-certifying names on usability grounds (they aren't human- or app-meaningful) and then presents its own scheme as better — but its scheme roots each user-chosen name in a *public key* namespace. Rooting a name in a key is precisely what makes it self-authenticating: the (key, name) pair binds the content to the namer. So CCN's model is "a self-authenticating name system, the same as Self-certifying Filesystem [Mazières] and as Tahoe-LAFS [Wilcox-O'Hearn]." The usability objection is real, but the solution converges on the same structure the objection was aimed at; CCN did not escape self-authenticating naming, it re-derived it. (Compare the [[content-address-versus-signature]] concept: content-addressing binds to the *bytes*; key-rooted naming binds to the *namer* — CCN uses the latter.)

**2. 'Trust' beyond the y-property is confusion.** CCN's "Evidence-Based Security" correctly captures *delegation* — a link says "the publisher intends this name to mean whatever that other publisher means by that name" — which Zooko praised as deeply important and correct. But the paper then reaches for a *network of trust*: "having decided to trust content A ... users may automatically trust the content A securely links to — e.g., its images, ads, source material." Zooko's objection is that there is no definition of "trust" that makes those sentences add anything over the **y-property**: the only thing a link to an image gives you is that *the image you receive is one the referring page found acceptable* (the resource is the one that namer meant by that name). It does *not* transfer any judgement of trustworthiness. And the paper's suggestion of comparing same-relative-path names under *different* keys (Alice's and Bob's identical `web_pages/front_page.html`) to derive information is "senseless" — they might mean the same thing or different things, and observing the shared path adds nothing beyond the per-name y-property guarantee.

## Bearing on Endo

The thread is a compact statement of two distinctions the SES/Endo and Tahoe/OCapN lineage depends on. First, **self-authenticating names** (name rooted in a key or a hash) are the recurring right answer for content and object identity, and the usability objection to them is answered by a *petname* layer on top, not by abandoning self-authentication — CCN's reinvention confirms the category keeps re-emerging. Second, the **y-property** is the precise, minimal statement of what a secure reference guarantees ("you get the resource the namer meant"), and it is *not* transitive trust — a warning that stays relevant wherever a system is tempted to promote "I trust A" into "I trust everything A links to." Endo's marshaled references and content-addressed bundles carry exactly the y-property guarantee and deliberately no more.
