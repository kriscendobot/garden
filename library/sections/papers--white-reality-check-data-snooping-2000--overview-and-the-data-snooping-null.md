Abstract: Data snooping occurs when a given set of data is used more than once for inference or model selection; when it does, a satisfactory result may be due to chance rather than to any merit in the method that produced it. White's stated purpose is to supply the missing tool: a straightforward, generally applicable procedure for testing the null hypothesis that **the best model encountered in a specification search has no predictive superiority over a given benchmark model**. This lets data snooping proceed with some confidence that one will not mistake chance for genuine skill. The null is formulated as a *multiple* hypothesis — the intersection of `l` one-sided hypotheses, where `l` is the number of models searched — and the goal is a method that does not lean on conservative Bonferroni-style bounds but directly delivers asymptotically correct p-values, controlling the *simultaneous* error rate.

**The hazard.** Whenever a "good" forecasting model is obtained by an extensive specification search, there is always the danger that the observed good performance results not from actual forecasting ability but from luck. Even when no exploitable forecasting relation exists, looking long enough and hard enough at a given set of data will often reveal one or more models that look good but are in fact useless. White's illustration is the newsletter scam: mail a free stock-market newsletter to a large group, telling half the market will rise and half that it will fall; the next week send only to those who got the correct call, again split in two; repeat. After several months a sizable group has received a string of perfect predictions and might pay for such "good" forecasts — skill that is pure survivorship of chance. The same question haunts the mutual fund or advisory service that advertises past performance: skill or luck? These are all data snooping (also called data mining).

**Why it is practically unavoidable.** The problem is endemic in time-series analysis because typically only a *single history* measuring a given phenomenon is available — there is often no other choice than to reuse the one dataset for both search and inference. Concern has a noble history (Cowles 1933 simulated whether advisory services beat chance; Lo and MacKinlay 1990 documented the biases; Leamer 1978, 1983 on the fragility of data-mined relationships), but White notes that none of the prior work provided a rigorously founded, generally applicable *test* of the best-model-versus-benchmark null. Standard multiple-comparison bounds (Bonferroni and its union-intersection improvements) were, as Hand (1998) put it, "not designed for the sheer numbers of candidate patterns generated by data mining."

**What "control the simultaneous error rate" means.** One could instead control the *average* rate of error (the frequency at which one finds "better" models); which is preferred is partly a matter of taste. Because the interest here is in *selecting and using an apparently best model* — not merely asking whether some model better than the benchmark might exist — White adopts the more stringent goal of controlling the simultaneous error rate under the null. This framing is what makes the Reality Check a search-aware test rather than a per-model one: it asks about the maximum apparent superiority across the whole searched collection, not about any single model in isolation.
